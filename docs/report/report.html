1What was made and for whom

The film tells a small family story in ten shots: waking up on Fasika expecting another holiday without my mother, and finding her home, making coffee for the family. It is for Henok, who was there that morning, and for Yishak and Betty.

Every part of the film comes from the storyboard:

  • Narration: my own lines in Amharic, voiced by OmniVoice with my own recorded voice as its sample (with my written consent), slowed to tempo 0.9.
  • Pictures: ten keyframes from FLUX.2 klein, conditioned on locked portraits of my family, which my parents and brother consented to.
  • Video: three shots turned into video with Wan 2.2 on Kaggle's free GPU; the other seven use slow camera moves.
  • Music: two cues from ACE-Step 1.5, a quiet “before” and a warm “Easter morning”, ducked under the voice.
  • Assembly: FFmpeg only: subtitles, loudness, the AI end card, and both aspect ratios.

3The hardest problems

Two problems took most of the three days: making the Amharic narration sound natural, and making the pictures culturally right and consistent.

A narrator that sounds like a person telling a story

The first radio cut (100 s, OmniVoice with a test sample) sounded robotic to all three testers. It took nine radio cuts to fix:

  1. Rewrote the lines by hand; that helped the story, not the voice (v2).
  2. Tried five other OmniVoice voices; all still sounded read from a script (EXPERIMENTS E6).
  3. Used my own recorded voice as OmniVoice's sample: the most natural Amharic (v3), but it sounded like me.
  4. Tried to keep my delivery with another voice, and failed. A synthetic sample gave nonsense at first, because Gemini read its English instruction aloud (FAILURES F15), and later the wrong pronunciation. Voice-design words still sounded like me (E7).
  5. Decided my own voice was acceptable, with my consent; slowed it to tempo 0.9, made lines 1 and 2 longer and tightened the pauses: radio cut v9, 62.7 s.

Lesson: OmniVoice takes the voice and the delivery from the same sample, so they can't be separated. In test 2 all three said the sound is much better.

Pictures that look like my family's Fasika, in one room

The first keyframes were wrong in ways only someone from the family could see: the ketema drawn as a lawn, a Western stove, mugs, a Christmas tree, a deformed girl (FAILURES F14). Described every object in detail: ketema as cut grass on the floor, the jebena, rekebot, sini cups, clay brazier and incense burner.

Then every shot invented a new room. Giving the model the shot 5 picture as the room fixed that (E8), but brought new problems with the people: an extra girl, the wrong family, my father and brother too small. Asking the same model to change one thing failed: it copied the picture almost exactly (E9). Gemini's image editing was not on the free tier (F18). What worked was repainting only marked areas with SDXL inpainting, while the code pastes every other pixel back unchanged (E10). Yishak's comment in test 2, that the characters, clothes and ketema look Ethiopian, is the evidence it worked.

4Suggested practices: what helped and what didn't

Two of the brief's practices beat my instinct: a reference sheet before the keyframes, and a JSON contract between the stages. Using reference images to fix pictures did not.

Helped: a reference sheet before the keyframes

My instinct: skip the reference sheet and describe the family again in each shot's prompt. Early on, asked whether every stage was really needed, because a separate portrait stage looked like extra time before the real work.

Evidence: spent nine rounds on the family's portraits before making any shot, and locked them (decision 31). Every keyframe is made with those portraits as reference images. The family is recognisably the same in shots 3 to 10, and in test 2 Yishak said the characters look Ethiopian, without being asked about them.

Helped: a JSON contract between the stages

My instinct: build it like my earlier project, a browser app where each picture is fixed by hand in an interface. One text file holding every decision looked like extra paperwork.

Evidence: every decision lives in storyboard.json, checked against a schema before each run: a picture chosen to keep is locked, an edit names the picture and the areas to repaint. After picking the music, make film rebuilt the film in one command and regenerated only the soundtrack and the two exports. A fresh clone on a second computer rebuilt the same film entirely from the cache.

Didn't help: reference images as the way to fix a picture

Reference images keep things the same, which is also why they couldn't change anything. The first keyframes copied the character sheet's layout, with plain backgrounds and inset portraits (F12). Later, given the chosen picture and asked to change only my father's and brother's size, the model changed 6 to 15% of the pixels and none of what was asked (E9). The fix was a different tool: masked repainting (E10).

5Ceiling work

Went beyond the brief in two places: an Amharic voice comparison, and picture tools the pipeline didn't have.

Picture tools for consistency and fixes

Added four ways to make a keyframe, each set in storyboard.json so anyone can repeat it:

  • Room reference (scene_from): an earlier shot's picture is given to the model as the room, so shots 6 to 10 share the room of shot 5 (E8).
  • Masked repainting: polygons in the storyboard mark what to change; every pixel outside them stays the same (E10). The same tool widens each 9:16 picture for the 16:9 film, keeping the original picture unchanged in the middle.
  • Locking (locked): a chosen picture is used as it is and never regenerated.
  • Candidates: several seeds for one shot; the one picked is reused from the cache, with no extra GPU time.
